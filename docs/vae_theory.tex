\begin{abstract}
The network in this repository is trained only on synthetic normal
transactions. At score time it rebuilds a row and compares that
reconstruction with a cutoff taken from other normal rows. This note
follows one transaction through that path, then derives the training
objective the code is approximating. The rows are generated here.
They are not taken from a real card-fraud dataset.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question, and the table it is asked on}

\texttt{ReconstructionScorer.score} answers a narrow question: is this
row harder to rebuild than the noisiest 5\% of a held-out normal sample?
The return value is a reconstruction score and a boolean,
\texttt{high\_risk}. Nothing in the return value is a probability of fraud.

\texttt{generate\_synthetic\_transactions} draws the table. Normal rows
are tied together on purpose: fee tracks amount, 24-hour rolling spend
falls as the gap since the last transaction grows, device type follows
distance from home, and merchant id follows spend. Fraud rows are a
minority (400 of 8{,}000, 5\%) and are shifted off those links by several
standard deviations. Recall of 1.0 on the notebook means the score
separated that planted gap. It is a check that the pipeline works under
the generator's assumptions.

The notebook split, all disjoint by \texttt{row\_id}:

\midrule

Calibration & 1200 & 0 & the 95th-percentile cutoff \\

\end{center}

\texttt{VAETrainer} receives the label tensor and does not pass it to
\texttt{TabularVAE} or \texttt{VAELoss}. The label is read again only in
\texttt{detection\_report}, after scores exist.

\section{Eighteen numbers}
\label{sec:input}

\texttt{ContinuousPreprocessor.fit} sees only the 5{,}200 training
normals. For each continuous column it stores the mean and the population
standard deviation of $\log(1+u)$. Later rows, including calibration,
validation, and anything passed to \texttt{score}, reuse those two
vectors. A non-finite or non-positive value is rejected. Zero is not
clamped to a small epsilon.

The notebook printed the fitted vectors rounded to three decimals:
\[
\begin{aligned}
m &= (3.246,\ 0.432,\ 4.068,\ 1.420,\ 8.813), \\
s &= (0.478,\ 0.179,\ 0.563,\ 0.375,\ 0.504).
\end{aligned}
\]
Applying that printed scaler to the ordinary check is an illustration,
not a dump of the tensor the model stored:
\[
\tilde{u}_{\text{ordinary}} \approx (-0.02,\ -0.15,\ -0.01,\ -0.09,\ -0.04).
\]
The same arithmetic on the shifted check gives roughly
$(5.0,\ 2.0,\ 4.9,\ 7.9,\ 4.9)$.
The recorded validation means, which \emph{are} model inputs averaged
over generated rows, agree with the picture: normals sat near
$(-0.04,\ -0.04,\ -0.03,\ 0.00,\ -0.01)$ and fraud near
$(4.94,\ 2.33,\ 4.84,\ 7.94,\ 4.70)$.

Categories are not scaled.

\texttt{encode\_categoricals} maps each token to an index.

\texttt{TabularVAE.encode} one-hots that index.
\texttt{m3} is index 3 of ten.
\texttt{contactless} is index 1.
Device order is \texttt{chip}, then \texttt{contactless}, then \texttt{online}.
The encoder input is the concatenation
\[
x =
\big[
\tilde{u}
\;\big|\;
e_{3}^{(10)}
\;\big|\;
e_{1}^{(3)}
\big]
\in \mathbb{R}^{18}
\]
for the ordinary check, and the analogous vector with $e_{8}^{(10)}$ and
$e_{0}^{(3)}$ for the shifted check. The 18 is $5+10+3$. Swapping vocab
order would train a different model than the one \texttt{score} applies.
An unknown token raises. It does not become a zero one-hot, and it is not
automatically high-risk.

\section{Six means and six log-variances}
\label{sec:encoder}

The encoder trunk is two affine maps with a ReLU after each:
\[
\begin{aligned}
h_{1} &= \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{1} x + b_{1}), & W_{1} &\in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 18}, \\
h_{2} &= \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{2} h_{1} + b_{2}), & W_{2} &\in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 64}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Two linear maps with no activation read $h_{2}$:
\[
\mu = W_{\mu} h_{2} + b_{\mu},
\qquad
\mathrm{logvar}
= \operatorname{clamp}(W_{\lambda} h_{2} + b_{\lambda},\, -10,\, 10).
\]
Both results have length 6. The clamp only stops $\exp(\mathrm{logvar})$
from overflowing. It is not a model of the data.

Those twelve numbers describe a region, not a point. The approximate
posterior is diagonal:
\[
q_{\phi}(z \mid x)
= \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu,\ \operatorname{diag}(\exp(\mathrm{logvar}))\big).
\]
$\mu$ is the center. $\exp(\mathrm{logvar}_{j})$ is the variance along
coordinate $j$. A confident encoder puts a tight blob on a normal row.
A row unlike the training normals can produce a center far from the
origin, a wide blob, or both. The network is not asked to emit a single
``correct'' $z$.

\section{Why training samples, and scoring does not}
\label{sec:reparam}

The reconstruction loss has to be computed at a code drawn from
$q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$, and the draw has to carry a gradient back into
$\mu$ and $\mathrm{logvar}$. Sampling $z$ inside the Gaussian and then
differentiating through the sample does not work: the draw itself is not
a function the autograd graph can see. The reparameterization writes the
same draw as a deterministic function of noise that does not depend on
$\phi$:
\[
\sigma = \exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{logvar}\right),
\qquad
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{6}),
\qquad
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon.
\]
\texttt{TabularVAE.forward} is that path: encode, sample, decode. The KL
term never needs $\epsilon$. It is a closed function of $\mu$ and
$\mathrm{logvar}$. The sample exists so a bad reconstruction can move the
Gaussian that produced the code.

\texttt{ReconstructionScorer} never calls \texttt{reparameterize}. It
decodes $\mu$. The ordinary check then scores the same way on every call.
Drawing a fresh $\epsilon$ would move the score even when the weights and
the row were fixed, and the cutoff stored from calibration would not refer
to the same quantity.

\section{Rebuilding numbers and rebuilding labels}
\label{sec:decoder}

The decoder maps $z \in \mathbb{R}^{6}$ through the same kind of trunk,
in the other direction:
\[
\begin{aligned}
g_{1} &= \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{3} z + b_{3}), & W_{3} &\in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 6}, \\
g_{2} &= \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{4} g_{1} + b_{4}), & W_{4} &\in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 64}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Three linear heads read $g_{2}$. None applies a softmax or a sigmoid.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.46\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Head & Shape & What it is asked to match \\
\midrule
\texttt{continuous\_head} & $64 \to 5$ &
$\tilde{u}$: scaled amount, fee, rolling spend, distance, seconds \\
merchant logits & $64 \to 10$ & the merchant index, via cross-entropy \\
device logits & $64 \to 3$ & the device index, via cross-entropy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The split is there because the two kinds of fields are not the same
mistake. Huber compares two real numbers in the scaled space. With
$\delta = 1$, a residual inside one training standard deviation is
quadratic, and a larger residual has constant slope:
\[
\ell_{1}(r) =
\begin{cases}
\tfrac{1}{2} r^{2} & \text{if } |r| \le 1, \\[0.3em]
|r| - \tfrac{1}{2} & \text{if } |r| > 1.
\end{cases}
\]
Squared error on a raw amount would let a few large but legitimate
tickets dominate the gradient. \texttt{log1p} and the z-score shrink that
problem. Huber is what remains so a tail residual still cannot grow like
$r^{2}$.

Cross-entropy compares a distribution over class indices with the index
that was observed. \texttt{F.cross\_entropy} applies the log-softmax to
the raw logits. A squared error on the merchant index would treat
\texttt{m9} as nine times \texttt{m1}. The vocabulary has no such order.
\texttt{m3} versus \texttt{m8} is an identity error, which is what the
merchant head is for. The same holds for \texttt{chip} versus
\texttt{contactless}.

On a normal row the continuous head is mostly checking the links in
$\tilde{u}$ (fee against amount, rolling spend against the gap). The
categorical heads are checking whether the merchant and device are the
ones that usually accompany that pattern. A shifted row can fail either
check or both.

\section{Why only normal rows are shown to the loss}
\label{sec:normal}

The weights never see a fraud row, so the decoder's job is only to
rebuild normal traffic inside the latent region the KL allows. After
training, every row still has an encoder output and a decoder output.
For a normal validation row, both were trained on rows like it, and the
residual is usually small. For a generated fraud row, the decoder's best
reconstruction is still a normal-looking combination: a fee that matches
the amount, a device that matches the distance, a merchant that matches
the spend. The inputs disagree with that combination, so Huber and
cross-entropy rise.

That is the whole detection idea. It fails in two directions that matter
later. A fraudulent row that happens to look normal in these seven fields
reconstructs easily and stays under the cutoff. A legitimate row that is
merely unfamiliar (a new distance, a rare but honest merchant) can land
above it. The score is conformity to the learned normal structure. It is
not evidence of intent.

If fraud were left in the training normals, the decoder would spend
capacity on those patterns and the gap would shrink. The notebook split
checks that train and calibration are entirely label 0. That check is not
available once the label is something you do not have.

\section{One number per row, then a frozen cutoff}
\label{sec:score}

Training and scoring use the same Huber delta and the same
$\lambda_{\mathrm{cont}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{cat}}$. They do not use
the same reduction.

\texttt{VAELoss} averages so a change in batch size does not rescale the
gradient. Huber is \texttt{F.huber\_loss} with \texttt{reduction="mean"},
which averages over the batch and over the five columns. Each head's
cross-entropy is averaged over the batch, then the two head-means are
added. The KL is summed over the six latent coordinates and then averaged
over the batch.

\texttt{reconstruction\_errors} sums inside the row instead. A transaction
needs one severity, not a batch mean:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Huber}_{\mathrm{sum}}
&= \sum_{j=1}^{5} \ell_{1}(\hat{u}_{j} - \tilde{u}_{j}), \\
\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{sum}}
&= \mathrm{CE}(\mathrm{logits}_{\mathrm{m}}, c_{\mathrm{m}})
+ \mathrm{CE}(\mathrm{logits}_{\mathrm{d}}, c_{\mathrm{d}}), \\
\mathrm{score}
&= 1 \cdot \mathrm{Huber}_{\mathrm{sum}}
+ 1 \cdot \mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{sum}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
KL is not in this sum. The decode is of $\mu$.

\texttt{fit\_threshold} runs that score on the 1{,}200 calibration normals
and stores the 95th percentile. The notebook printed 2.1162
(the float kept on the scorer was $2.116162061691284$).
\texttt{high\_risk} is $\mathrm{score} \ge \mathrm{threshold}$.
Later calls read the stored float. They do not take a new percentile of
the request, and they do not take one of the validation set. The
calibration rows are not in the classification report.

On the recorded run the score distributions were:

\midrule

Fraud scores started at 13.189. All 400 sat above 2.1162, so fraud recall
is 1.0 and fraud precision is 0.883. The precision is below 1 because
some validation normals also crossed the line: the notebook's flag rate
on them was 0.044. That is 4.4\%, close to 5\% and not equal to it.
The cutoff is an order statistic of one sample of 1{,}200 normals. The
validation normals are a different sample. Their own 95th percentile was
2.040, below the calibration cutoff, so fewer than one in twenty of them
were flagged. The two rates would match only if the two empirical
percentiles were the same number.

The two hand-written checks, which are actual \texttt{score} outputs:
the ordinary row scored 0.052 (continuous error 0.051, both categorical
errors near 0) and was not high-risk. The shifted row scored 25.95
(continuous 15.90, merchant 3.79, device 6.26) and was high-risk.
Those categorical pieces are cross-entropies of the true class, not
accuracies. A device term of 6.26 means the \texttt{chip} logit was far
below the alternatives, which is what a far-from-home chip purchase looks
like to a decoder trained on local chip use.

\section{The objective, after the path is clear}
\label{sec:elbo}

An autoencoder only minimizes reconstruction. With enough capacity it can
give a strange row its own latent island and rebuild it perfectly. The
island does not have to lie near the codes of ordinary rows, and a point
halfway between two islands does not have to decode to anything
normal-looking. A VAE adds a cost for that. The encoder must place
$q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ near $\mathcal{N}(0, I_{6})$, so the codes of normal
rows share one region. Interpolation inside that region is then a mix of
normal reconstructions rather than a jump between memorized points.

That is the reason this repo uses the KL term. It is not evidence that a
VAE will beat an autoencoder on every anomaly problem. If the bottleneck
is already tight, or if the anomalies are obvious in the input, the KL
may change little. If $\beta$ is large, reconstruction of the normals
themselves gets worse and the cutoff becomes a blur.

The marginal likelihood makes the same point in one integral. A decoder
$p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ and a prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I_{6})$ define
\[
\log p_{\theta}(x)
= \log \int p_{\theta}(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz.
\]
The integral is over $\mathbb{R}^{6}$, and the decoder is a stack of
ReLUs, so there is no closed form to differentiate. Insert the encoder
density, which integrates to one, and apply Jensen's inequality to the
logarithm:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo}
\log p_{\theta}(x)
\ge
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}[\log p_{\theta}(x \mid z)]
- \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big).
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is the ELBO. The expectation is the reconstruction
term: the code just drawn has to explain the row. The KL is the pressure
that keeps those codes in one region.

\texttt{VAELoss} does not implement the negative of \eqref{eq:elbo} with
a single density. There is no learned observation variance, and Huber is
not a Gaussian log-likelihood. Cross-entropy on a head \emph{is} the
negative log-likelihood of a categorical distribution over that head's
classes. The KL \emph{is} the exact diagonal-Gaussian formula, averaged
over the batch:
\[
\mathrm{KL}_{i}
= -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{6}
\Big(
1 + \mathrm{logvar}_{i,j}
- \mu_{i,j}^{2}
- \exp(\mathrm{logvar}_{i,j})
\Big),
\quad
\mathrm{KL}
= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathrm{KL}_{i}.
\]
The quantity that is differentiated is a weighted surrogate:
\[
\mathrm{total}
= \lambda_{\mathrm{cont}}\,\mathrm{Huber}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{cat}}\,(\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{m}}+\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{d}})
+ \beta\,\mathrm{KL},
\]
with $\lambda_{\mathrm{cont}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{cat}} = 1$ and
$\beta = 0.1$. Huber plus the two cross-entropies stand in for
$-\mathbb{E}[\log p_{\theta}(x \mid z)]$. They are not one joint density
evaluated at $x$.

$\beta = 0.1$ is the trade. The ELBO uses weight 1 on the KL. That weight
is large enough, early in training, to collapse $q_{\phi}$ toward the
prior before the decoder has learned the normal links. A smaller $\beta$
lets reconstruction move first. It does not keep the KL small forever.
At epoch 25 the printed pieces were Huber 0.2116, cross-entropy 0.0861,
and KL 5.8136, so the weighted terms were 0.2116, 0.0861, and
$\beta \cdot \mathrm{KL} = 0.5814$. The regularizer was the largest term
in the training loss. The score in Section~\ref{sec:score} still omits it.

Leaving KL out is a choice of signal, not a claim that posterior anomaly
scores are useless. One could threshold the KL itself, or the likelihood
of $z$ under the prior, or a density estimated on the normal codes.
Those scores answer ``how strange is the latent distribution for this
row.'' This repository answers ``how badly did the normal-trained decoder
rebuild this row,'' and the calibration percentile is a percentile of
that rebuild error only.

\section{What the perfect recall does not mean}
\label{sec:limits}

Generated fraud is easy for this score. The mean scaled fraud row sits
several standard deviations from the normal cloud, and the minimum fraud
score in validation was 13.2 against a cutoff of 2.12. A bug that dropped
the fraud rows into the normal cloud would have failed this test.
Passing it does not measure performance on real payments, because no real
payments were used.

Other limits are structural:

\begin{itemize}
\item The category sets are closed. A new merchant id is rejected in
\texttt{score}. It is not given a score and it is not labeled fraud.
\item Each row is independent. There is no cardholder, no account, and no
sequence. The gap and the rolling spend are columns, not a hidden state
carried across time.
\item The cutoff drifts when normal behavior drifts. 2.1162 belongs to
this calibration window. A later window needs its own percentile, fit on
normals that were not used to train, and then frozen again.
\item The score is not a probability. Crossing 2.1162 means ``at or above
the stored percentile,'' which on this run flagged 4.4\% of a second
normal sample and 100\% of the synthetic fraud.
\item Normal training data that secretly contains fraud teaches the
decoder to rebuild those fraud patterns. The synthetic split forbids that.
A production extract has to enforce it some other way.
\end{itemize}

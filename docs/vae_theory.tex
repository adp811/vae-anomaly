\begin{abstract}
The model learns what a normal transaction looks like, then flags a row
it cannot rebuild. This note walks that idea through the two notebooks
in the repo, with the numbers those runs actually printed.
The synthetic table is generated here. The Kaggle table is the public
2013 card file. Neither result is a production false-positive budget.
The derivations sit in Appendix~\ref{sec:math}.
\end{abstract}

\section{The idea}

Learn what normal transactions look like. Flag what the model cannot rebuild.

The fraud label never enters the loss. It is used only to keep fraud out
of the training and calibration windows, and later to score the holdout.
That is normal-only, or semi-supervised, anomaly detection. Calling it
unsupervised would be wrong.

\section{An autoencoder is a summary}

Picture one transaction as a short form someone has to fill in, and then
redraw the original from that form alone. The encoder writes the summary.
In both notebooks the summary is six numbers. The decoder tries to redraw
the amount, the fee, the merchant, and the rest from those six numbers.
Training punishes a bad redraw.

Six numbers cannot store every detail of an 18- or 30-wide row, so the
network keeps the patterns that show up again and again in the rows it is
shown. Those rows are normal. An unusual row asks for a summary the
decoder never practiced. The redraw comes out looking more normal than
the input, and the gap between them is the score.

This fails in both directions. Fraud that looks ordinary in the columns
you kept is easy to redraw and stays under the cutoff. A legitimate row
that is merely new can score high. If fraud was mixed into the training
normals, the decoder learns to redraw that too, and the gap goes quiet.

A plain autoencoder can also cheat. Nothing tells it where the six
numbers should live. It can park a strange row on its own island, far
from the summaries of ordinary rows, and still redraw that island
perfectly. The training rows look fine. A point halfway between two
islands need not decode to anything that looks like a transaction.
``How typical is this code?'' has no answer, because there is no map of
typical codes. The variational piece is that map.

\section{What ``variational'' adds}

Instead of pinning each transaction to one exact code, the encoder names
a small region: a center, and a width along each of the six directions.
A confident row gets a tight region. An unfamiliar row can sit far from
the origin, or spread out, or both.

The KL term is a pull that keeps the map tidy. Reconstruction is happy
to give a row its own far-away code if that makes the redraw better.
The KL charges for a region that wanders away from a simple shared blob,
a standard bell curve centered at the origin. Training stops at a
compromise. Nearby normal transactions end up in one neighborhood, and a
point in that neighborhood is a place the decoder has been asked to turn
into a normal-looking row. Figure~\ref{fig:forces} is the cartoon.
It is not a plot from either notebook.

The tidy map is the reason to use a variational autoencoder here.
It is not a claim that this model always beats a plain autoencoder at
finding fraud. If the bottleneck is already tight, or the anomalies are
obvious in the raw columns, the extra pull may change little.

\section{Randomness the model can still learn from}

Training has to try a code drawn from that fuzzy region, and the draw
has to teach the center and the width. If the program samples a code and
then treats the sample as a fixed number, the learner can improve the
decoder but cannot move the region that produced the code. The randomness
was a wall.

The fix is to treat the randomness as an input. Draw noise that does not
depend on the network, then build the code from the center, the width,
and that noise:
\[
z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \epsilon .
\]
Read it as: start at the center $\mu$, and step by the noise $\epsilon$
scaled by the width $\sigma$. A hand calculation, not a row from either
notebook: center 1, width 0.5, noise $-1$, so the code used on that step
is $0.5$. Because the formula is ordinary arithmetic, a better redraw can
nudge the center and the width. The appendix writes the same step with
the log-variance the code actually stores.

Scoring does not take that random step. It redraws from the center alone.
A new coin flip would change the score of a fixed transaction, and a
cutoff fit on one set of flips would not match the next set.

\section{One transaction, end to end}
\label{sec:walk}

The synthetic notebook's checks below were typed by hand. They are not
rows the generator sampled. The ordinary one sits near the generator's
normal medians. The shifted one breaks the same links the fraud sampler
breaks: a large amount with a small fee, a long gap with high rolling
spend, a far-from-home chip purchase, a rare merchant.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Field & Ordinary & Shifted \\
\midrule
amount & 24.5 & 280 \\
fee & 0.50 & 1.2 \\
rolling spend, 24h & 57 & 900 \\
distance from home, km & 3 & 80 \\
seconds since last transaction & 6600 & 80000 \\
merchant & m3 & m8 \\
device & contactless & chip \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Five positive numbers are logged with $\log(1+u)$ and then z-scored.
The mean and spread of that transform are fit on the 5{,}200 training
normals only, and reused everywhere else. Merchant ids \texttt{m0}--\texttt{m9}
and the devices \texttt{chip}, \texttt{contactless}, \texttt{online}
become one-hot blocks in that order. The encoder therefore sees
$5+10+3=18$ inputs. Merchant and device are categories. Scoring them with
squared error would pretend that \texttt{m3} sits halfway between
\texttt{m2} and \texttt{m4}. There is no such order.

The encoder turns those 18 inputs into six centers and six widths.
The notebook does not print those twelve numbers for these two checks.
What it prints is the redraw error after the decoder has tried to recover
the five scaled numbers, the merchant, and the device. Numeric error uses
a Huber penalty, gentle on small misses and linear on large ones, so one
huge amount cannot dominate. Category error is cross-entropy: did the
decoder put its probability on the merchant and device that were actually
there?

The score adds those pieces inside the row. Training, by contrast,
averages them, so a bigger batch does not inflate the step.
\[
\mathrm{score} = \text{sum of the per-feature errors}.
\]
The tidy-map pull is not in that sum. Other systems do threshold it.
This one does not. The cutoff is a percentile of the same sum on held-out
normals, then frozen. A row is flagged when its score is at or above
that stored number.

On the ordinary check the score was about 0.052, almost all of it from
the numeric columns, and the cutoff was 2.1162, so the row was not
flagged. On the shifted check the score was 25.95: about 15.90 from the
five numeric terms, 3.79 from the merchant, and 6.26 from the device.
The device term is large because a far-from-home chip purchase disagrees
with a decoder trained on local chip use. 25.95 is above 2.1162, so the
row was flagged.

The Kaggle notebook is the same loop with a different row. There is no
merchant and no device, so there is no category term. The thirty inputs
are \texttt{Time}, the dataset's anonymized components \texttt{V1}--\texttt{V28},
and \texttt{Amount}. Amount is logged, then scaled. Time and the
components are scaled as they are: Time is a clock starting at 0, and the
components are already signed. The split follows time rather than a
shuffle: first 60\% of rows for training, next 20\% for calibration,
last 20\% for the mixed holdout. The operating cutoff is the 99th
percentile of calibration scores, not the 95th.

\section{Knobs, including beta}
\label{sec:knobs}

Beta is the price of a code that leaves the shared blob. In the ideal
formula that price has weight 1. These notebooks turn the weight down.

Turn it toward zero and the tidy-map pull vanishes. You are close to a
plain autoencoder that still happens to emit a center and a width.
The summary can improve, and the islands can come back. That is not
automatically a bad detector. It does give up the reason for the
variational piece.

Turn it up and the blob wins. Every transaction is assigned roughly the
same uninformative region, the decoder redraws something close to the
average normal row, and the score stops distinguishing anyone. That is
posterior collapse: the encoder collapsed onto the prior, and the score
went quiet because the code went quiet.

The synthetic run used beta 0.1, a trunk of width 64, and 25 epochs.
Epoch 25 printed numeric error 0.2116, category error 0.0861, and a KL
of 5.8136. Weighted, the tidy-map term is $0.1 \times 5.8136 = 0.5814$,
larger than the two reconstruction pieces together (0.2977), but it had
not erased them. Planted fraud was still easy to flag. On that table,
0.1 was a compromise, not a collapse.

The Kaggle columns are a harder case. Once each column is scaled, they
are only weakly tied, and six numbers cannot faithfully redraw all thirty.
A diagnostic fit on the same training window, seed 42, width 64, and
beta 0.1, reached numeric error 0.3456 and a KL of 0.0001 by epoch 4.
Predicting the scaled training mean --- a vector of zeros --- scores
0.3469 on that window. The centers on a training batch had standard
deviation about 0.003. The code was unused.

The notebook therefore uses beta 0.01 and width 128. Epoch 12 printed
numeric error 0.1382, well under 0.3469, so the code is doing some work.
The weighted KL on that epoch is $0.01 \times 7.9357 = 0.0794$, smaller
than the numeric term. The curve was still falling slightly at the end
(0.1440 at epoch 9, 0.1382 at epoch 12). Twelve epochs is the budget of
the run, not a claim that training had finished. Two usual remedies,
raising beta gradually and allowing each coordinate a small free KL
budget, are not implemented.

\section{Limits, briefly}

The synthetic fraud is far from the normal pattern on purpose.
The Kaggle file is a real public extract from one European card program
in 2013, and its main columns are already someone else's principal
components. Ranking this holdout does not say what a later year or
another issuer would do.

Unseen merchant ids are rejected, not scored. There is no cardholder and
no sequence; time fields are columns on one row. A wrong ``normal'' label
teaches the decoder to rebuild fraud. The cutoffs 2.1162 and 34.7925
belong to the windows that produced them, and they are not probabilities.
Beta was held fixed, and the Kaggle numeric error was still inching down
at epoch 12.

A mixed table such as IEEE-CIS would use the synthetic shape --- numbers
plus ids --- at a real vocabulary size, with embeddings once one-hot
merchant columns stop fitting. Raising beta gradually is the first
training change if a new table collapses the way beta 0.1 did here.
A later week needs its own normal calibration window, not a new model
class. Serving is the frozen scaler, the weights, and the stored cutoff.

This appendix is the derivation behind the story above.

\subsection{Symbols}

$x$ is one observed row. $z$ is its code. $\theta$ is the decoder's
weights. $\phi$ is the encoder's weights.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.74\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$p(z)$ & Prior. Here $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, fixed before training. \\
$p_{\theta}(x\mid z)$ & Decoder: how a code would generate a row. \\
$p_{\theta}(x)$ & Marginal likelihood of a row. An integral over every $z$. \\
$p_{\theta}(z\mid x)$ & True posterior. Intractable, because of the marginal. \\
$q_{\phi}(z\mid x)$ & Encoder: a tractable stand-in for the posterior. \\
$\mu,\mathrm{logvar}$ & Mean and log-variance of the encoder Gaussian. \\
$\sigma$ & $\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{logvar})$, the standard deviation. \\
$\epsilon$ & Noise from $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, independent of $\phi$. \\
$\operatorname{ELBO}$ & Evidence lower bound. The ideal training objective. \\
$\beta$ & Weight on the KL term. The ideal ELBO uses $\beta=1$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{The integral}

A latent-variable model defines the probability of a row by averaging
the decoder over every code, weighted by the prior:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal}
p_{\theta}(x)
= \int p_{\theta}(x\mid z)\, p(z)\, dz.
\end{equation}
If you could compute this, you would train $\theta$ by maximizing
$\log p_{\theta}(x)$. You cannot. The code is six-dimensional, and the
decoder is linear maps and ReLUs, not a density with a closed integral.
A grid over $\mathbb{R}^{6}$ is not a serious quadrature. Sampling from
the prior is the wrong average once training has started: most prior
samples are codes the decoder does not associate with this $x$.

The useful sampler would be the posterior,
\[
p_{\theta}(z\mid x)
= \frac{p_{\theta}(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{p_{\theta}(x)},
\]
and the denominator is \eqref{eq:marginal}. The distribution you need in
order to train is defined using the quantity you are trying to compute.
Variational inference replaces that posterior with a family you choose.
Here the family is a diagonal Gaussian. It is not the true posterior.

\subsection{The ELBO, two ways}
\label{sec:elbo}

\paragraph{The identity.}
From $p_{\theta}(x,z)=p_{\theta}(x\mid z)\,p(z)$ and
$\log p_{\theta}(x)=\log p_{\theta}(x,z)-\log p_{\theta}(z\mid x)$.
The left side does not depend on $z$, so any average over
$q_{\phi}(z\mid x)$ leaves it unchanged:
\begin{align}
\log p_{\theta}(x)
&= \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}\!\left[\log p_{\theta}(x,z)-\log q_{\phi}(z\mid x)\right] \nonumber\\
&\quad + \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}\!\left[\log q_{\phi}(z\mid x)-\log p_{\theta}(z\mid x)\right].
\label{eq:identity}
\end{align}
The second expectation is
$\mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_{\theta}(z\mid x)\big)$.
It is zero when the two distributions match, and positive otherwise.
The first expectation is therefore a lower bound on $\log p_{\theta}(x)$,
and the gap is exactly how far the encoder is from the true posterior.

Expand the joint:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elbo}
\operatorname{ELBO}(x)
= \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}[\log p_{\theta}(x\mid z)]
 - \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:identity} then reads
\[
\log p_{\theta}(x)
= \operatorname{ELBO}(x)
+ \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_{\theta}(z\mid x)\big).
\]
The expectation says the code you just drew has to explain the row.
The KL against the prior says the encoder cannot use an arbitrary island
to do it. When $q_{\phi}$ equals the true posterior, the gap is zero and
the ELBO equals $\log p_{\theta}(x)$.

\paragraph{Jensen.}
Insert $q_{\phi}$, which integrates to one, into \eqref{eq:marginal}:
\[
\log p_{\theta}(x)
= \log \int q_{\phi}(z\mid x)\,
  \frac{p_{\theta}(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_{\phi}(z\mid x)}\, dz.
\]
The log of an average is at least the average of the log, because
$\log$ is concave:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jensen}
\log p_{\theta}(x)
\ge
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}\!\left[
\log p_{\theta}(x\mid z)+\log p(z)-\log q_{\phi}(z\mid x)
\right].
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is \eqref{eq:elbo}. Jensen says the ELBO is a lower
bound. The identity names the missing piece, which Jensen hides inside
the inequality.

Training never touches $\log p_{\theta}(x)$. It maximizes a minibatch
estimate of the ELBO, or the weighted surrogate written below.

\subsection{Closed-form KL for a diagonal Gaussian}
\label{sec:kl}

The encoder emits $\mu$ and $\mathrm{logvar}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, and
\[
q_{\phi}(z\mid x)
= \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu,\ \operatorname{diag}(\exp(\mathrm{logvar}))\big).
\]
Coordinate $j$ has variance $\sigma_{j}^{2}=\exp(\mathrm{logvar}_{j})$.
The prior $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ has the same independence, so the KL is the
sum of one-dimensional KLs.

Drop the index. Up to the shared constant $-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)$,
\begin{align*}
\log q(z)
&= -\tfrac{1}{2}\log \sigma^{2} - \frac{(z-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}, \\
\log p(z)
&= -\frac{z^{2}}{2}.
\end{align*}
Under $q$, $(z-\mu)^{2}$ has expectation $\sigma^{2}$, so the second term
of $\log q$ averages to $\tfrac{1}{2}$. And
$\mathbb{E}_{q}[z^{2}]=\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2}$, so
$\mathbb{E}_{q}[z^{2}/2]=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu^{2}$.
The KL is the difference of those expectations:
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)
&= -\tfrac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}
   + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mu^{2} \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}-1-\log\sigma^{2}\big).
\end{align*}
The $2\pi$ terms cancelled. With $\sigma^{2}=\exp(\mathrm{logvar})$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl-1d}
\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)
= -\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1+\mathrm{logvar}-\mu^{2}-\exp(\mathrm{logvar})\Big).
\end{equation}
Sum over coordinates, then average over the batch so a short batch and a
long batch report the same scale:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl-code}
\mathrm{KL}
= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
\left(
-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
\big(1+\mathrm{logvar}_{i,j}-\mu_{i,j}^{2}-\exp(\mathrm{logvar}_{i,j})\big)
\right).
\end{equation}
Two checks: $\mu=0$ and $\mathrm{logvar}=0$ give KL $0$, the encoder
matching the prior. $\mu=1$ and $\mathrm{logvar}=0$ give $\tfrac{1}{2}$.
The code clamps log-variance to $[-10,10]$ only so the exponential cannot
overflow. The clamp is not a model of the data.

\subsection{Reparameterization, with the gradient}

The reconstruction term averages a function of a sample from $q_{\phi}$.
A Gaussian sampler that returns a constant blocks the gradient into
$\mu$ and $\mathrm{logvar}$. Write the same draw as arithmetic on noise
that does not depend on $\phi$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reparam}
\sigma=\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{logvar}\right),
\qquad
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\qquad
z=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon.
\end{equation}
Then $\partial z/\partial\mu = 1$ and $\partial z/\partial\sigma = \epsilon$.
On the one-coordinate example in the main text, noise $-1$ means a push
that wants a larger $z$ also shrinks $\sigma$ on that draw. The next draw
can push $\sigma$ the other way. Across a batch the width learns a scale,
rather than chasing one sample. The KL never needs $\epsilon$; it is
already \eqref{eq:kl-code}. \texttt{TabularVAE.forward} is encode, then
\eqref{eq:reparam}, then decode. Scoring decodes $\mu$.

\subsection{Which likelihood becomes which loss}

The reconstruction term in \eqref{eq:elbo} is an expected log-likelihood.
The loss you minimize is its negative, plus the KL.

A Gaussian decoder with fixed variance has log-likelihood
$-\|x-\hat{x}\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})$ plus a constant. Minimizing mean
squared error is that likelihood. The variance only rescales it.

A class index matches a categorical likelihood. The decoder emits logits.
Cross-entropy is the negative log of the observed class under the softmax.
\texttt{F.cross\_entropy} applies the log-softmax. A softmax in the module
as well would train the wrong objective. Squared error on the class index
would invent an order the vocabulary does not have.

The notebooks replace Gaussian MSE with Huber loss. With $\delta=1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:huber}
\ell_{1}(r)=
\begin{cases}
\tfrac{1}{2} r^{2} & \text{if } |r|\le 1,\\
|r|-\tfrac{1}{2} & \text{if } |r|>1.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Inside one scaled standard deviation the penalty is quadratic, the shape
of a fixed-variance Gaussian negative log-likelihood. Outside, it is
linear, the shape of a Laplace negative log-likelihood. The density whose
negative log is \eqref{eq:huber} is therefore Gaussian in the center and
exponential in the tails. No variance parameter is fit. $\delta=1$ puts
the kink at one training standard deviation after scaling.

\texttt{VAELoss} does not differentiate the negative of \eqref{eq:elbo}
under one joint density. It differentiates
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:train-loss}
\mathrm{total}
= \lambda_{\mathrm{cont}}\,\mathrm{Huber}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{cat}}\,(\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{m}}+\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{d}})
+ \beta\,\mathrm{KL},
\end{equation}
with both lambdas equal to 1. Reductions:

\begin{itemize}
\item Huber: mean over the batch and over the continuous columns.
\item Each cross-entropy: mean over the batch. The head-means are then
  added, so each categorical column contributes once. On the Kaggle model
  there are no heads, the categorical term is a zero tensor, and
  $\lambda_{\mathrm{cat}}$ multiplies zero.
\item KL: \eqref{eq:kl-code}.
\end{itemize}

A uniform guess on the synthetic heads would score
$\ln 10 + \ln 3 \approx 3.401$. Epoch 1 printed cross-entropy 3.2698,
just under chance. Epoch 25 printed 0.0861. The categories were being
reconstructed by the end of that run.

Scoring uses the same $\delta$ and the same lambdas, summed inside the
row, with no KL:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
\mathrm{score}
= \lambda_{\mathrm{cont}}\sum_{j}\ell_{1}(\hat{u}_{j}-\tilde{u}_{j})
 + \lambda_{\mathrm{cat}}\sum_{\mathrm{heads}}\mathrm{CE}(\mathrm{logits}, c).
\end{equation}
Arithmetic on the definition, not a data row: Huber pieces 0.2 and 0.4.
Training averages them to 0.3. The score adds them to 0.6.
The synthetic score has five Huber terms and two cross-entropies.
The Kaggle score has thirty Huber terms and no cross-entropy.
A large KL, the prior density of the center, or a density fit on normal
codes would be alternative anomaly signals. They were not the ones
implemented in \texttt{score.py}.
